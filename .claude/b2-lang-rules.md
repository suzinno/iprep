# B2 language rules

Write or rewrite any text so a CEFR B2 reader or listener understands it on the first pass, without changing what it says.

**Scope.** These rules apply to text written from scratch and to text rewritten from an existing source. Sections A, B and D are the same either way. Where the two paths differ — rules 15, 18 and 19 — the rule names both.

## The core rule

Simplifying changes the words, never the content. Every fact, condition, qualifier, number and exception stays in. If a plainer word changes the claim, it is the wrong word.

For new text the same rule points at your own draft: the plain wording must carry every condition the technical wording would have carried.

## A. Words

**1. Keep the domain's own terms; make the words around them common.**

A reader in the field expects its terms (in software "timeout", "threshold", "repository" and so on). Everything else uses frequent, everyday words.

```
"holds"             not "pins"
"fails completely"  not "fails wholesale"
"work together"     not "compose"
"start"             not "commence"
```

**2. Use one name for one thing.**

Once something has a name, keep it. Switching between synonyms ("the tool", "the utility", "the mechanism") makes a B2 reader think they are different things.

**3. Expand abbreviations the first time they appear, unless the audience uses them every day.**

**4. Avoid idioms and figurative phrases.**

```
"is overloaded"  not "is drowning"
"starts to fail" not "goes south"
```

## B. Sentences

**5. One idea per sentence.**

A sentence that carries a "because", a "so" and a dash is two or three sentences.

**6. Keep sentences short: about 20 words, rarely over 25.**

A single idea that needs more is usually a list or two steps.

**7. Use a verb, not a noun phrase.**

```
"It only looks at the overall failure rate."
not "It decides on the aggregate of recent outcomes within its scope."
```

**8. No inversion or formal connectors.**

```
not "Nor is it...", "Provided that...", "On the evidence of...", "Notwithstanding...", "Hence..."
```

Join ideas with: because, so, but, when, if, and, also, then.

**9. Make every pronoun point at one thing.**

If "it" or "this" could mean two nouns from the last sentence, repeat the noun. Never let "it" stand for different things in neighbouring sentences.

**10. Prefer the active voice when the doer matters.**

```
"The server rejects the request."  not  "The request is rejected."
```

Passive is fine when the doer is unknown or unimportant.

## C. Keeping the meaning

**11. Keep every condition word.**

"at or above", "can", "may", "by default", "usually", "only if", version or date scopes, stated exceptions. Dropping one turns a true claim into a false one.

**12. Do not turn a necessary condition into a sufficient one.**

```
"can only stop at a checkpoint"  is not  "stops at every checkpoint"
```

**13. Do not make a claim stronger or weaker.**

"often" does not become "always"; "reduces" does not become "removes".

**14. Keep the reason and the consequence.**

A fact without its "because" or its "so what" is harder to understand, not easier. Simple sentences may split the reason out, but it must stay.

**15. Add no new facts.**

*Rewriting:* every claim in the rewrite must trace to the original text. Do not fill gaps from memory; name them separately.

*Writing new text:* every claim must be one you can support, and you name what supports it. A claim you cannot check is marked as unverified, never written as settled.

## D. Making it shorter (only when a length limit applies)

**16. Cut in this order:**

```
1) repetition and restated conclusions
2) examples, when a rule already states the point
3) side cases
```

Never cut: part of the question being answered, the reason something is true, or the consequence the text sets up.

**17. Simpler language usually takes more words.** If the limit cannot be met with only protected content left, report the overage. Do not cut meaning to hit a number.

## E. Checking the result

**18. List the claims before rewriting.**

*Rewriting:* list the claims in the original.

*Writing new text:* list the claims your draft makes.

**19. Compare claim by claim.**

*Rewriting:* compare the original against the rewrite. Each claim is either kept with the same meaning, or deliberately cut (say so). Flag any claim that got stronger, weaker or lost a condition.

*Writing new text:* check each claim is correct as written. Flag every claim you could not verify, and name the source of the ones you checked.

**20. Check the language sentence by sentence against A and B.** Quote each sentence that breaks a rule, with its replacement.

**21. Have someone other than the writer do 19 and 20** (a second person or an independent reviewer). A writer checking their own text finds what they hoped to find.

**22. Count words with a tool, not by eye, when a limit applies.**
